Sensitivity to the annotation boundary was examined by eroding the full ROI once using a three-dimensional six-neighbor structuring element. Erosion operated on the supplied voxel grid and did not represent a fixed distance in millimeters. We recalculated the same eight MRI features within the eroded region, retaining the original image-specific percentile normalization parameters and the discretization boundaries derived from the full ROI. This kept the intensity and binning reference consistent when changing the region. Eroded MRI and Fusion models used the original examination set, patient groups and five-fold assignments, with their preprocessing and regression fitted within each training fold under the same fixed settings. The clinical inputs were unchanged. Comparisons included eroded Fusion versus M1 and eroded versus original Fusion, using paired patient-cluster bootstrap intervals conditional on the saved OOF predictions. This perturbation assessed dependence on the supplied boundary; it did not establish segmentation accuracy. The original ROI remained the primary analysis.

To examine sensitivity to fold assignment, we repeated stratified five-fold validation by patient group on the full imaging cohort using 20 seeds fixed as the consecutive integers 20261029 through 20261048. The first partition reproduced the primary analysis. Within each repetition, M0, M1, MRI and Fusion shared the same examinations and folds, and preprocessing and regression were fitted separately within each training fold. Each examination received one OOF prediction per model per repetition. We summarized performance and the Fusion-minus-M1 differences using the median, quartiles and observed minimum and maximum, and counted repetitions with positive differences. The protocol required recording any failed repetitions without replacing their seeds. Because all repetitions reused the same patients, their distributions described sensitivity to partitioning and were not confidence intervals or independent replications. We did not pool repeated predictions as independent observations, average them into an ensemble or choose the highest-performing repetition to replace the primary estimate. Model settings and the classification threshold remained fixed.

A separate sensitivity analysis retained one examination per patient, selecting the smallest numeric session suffix available in the imaging subset, with the complete examination identifier resolving ties. Selection used neither diagnostic labels nor model predictions. This rule identified the earliest numbered available session, without establishing the first clinical visit or the earliest MRI in the source database. We rebuilt patient-grouped five-fold partitions with seed 20261029 and refitted the four fixed models within these training folds. Patient bootstrap sampling used 2000 attempts with seed 20261101 and fixed OOF probabilities. Percentile intervals for ROC AUC, treatment-related-change AP, Brier score, logarithmic loss and their Fusion-minus-M1 differences followed the conditional interpretation above. Draws containing only one diagnostic class were ineligible and would not be replaced. The selected cohort differed from the primary cohort in examination composition, class distribution and training data. Accordingly, differences between their performance estimates could not be attributed solely to removing repeated scans, and the single-examination analysis did not replace the primary comparison.
