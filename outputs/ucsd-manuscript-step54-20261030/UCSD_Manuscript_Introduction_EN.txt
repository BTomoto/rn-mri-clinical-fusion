Treatment follow-up in high-grade glioma requires clinicians to interpret magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) abnormalities in the context of previous therapy. For glioblastoma, contemporary management combines surgery, radiotherapy and systemic treatment, while subsequent imaging contributes to assessment of disease status and further management (18). New or increasing contrast enhancement after treatment may indicate tumor activity, but can also reflect treatment effects. An apparent increase in lesion burden therefore does not, by itself, resolve the underlying diagnosis. Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) 2.0 addresses this problem through standardized response assessment and, in relevant early post-radiotherapy settings, confirmation of progression by repeat imaging or unequivocal histopathological evidence (19). Such requirements emphasize the importance of temporal and clinical context when interpreting a suspicious examination. They also motivate methods that can summarize information already present in the scan and assess whether it adds to available clinical information. The immediate research question is whether that combination improves discrimination of the status assigned to an examination, providing evidence for subsequent evaluation rather than replacing the broader diagnostic process.

The difficulty is partly one of outcome definition. Pseudoprogression commonly refers to an apparent radiological worsening related to treatment that subsequently stabilizes or regresses without a new antitumor treatment. Radiation necrosis represents a different form of treatment injury, often discussed as a later complication; these terms should not be treated as interchangeable with every nonspecific post-treatment abnormality (18,20). RANO 2.0 implementation guidance further distinguishes enhancing, nonenhancing and mixed imaging presentations and the circumstances requiring confirmation scans (21). These distinctions matter when comparing diagnostic studies, because a model trained to identify pseudoprogression may address a narrower target than a model separating tumor from a broad treatment-change category. Reference diagnoses also vary: pathological assessment, clinical evolution and serial imaging provide different forms of evidence, and even pathological differentiation can remain difficult (18). The public UCSD-PTGBM collection includes residual or recurrent tumor and several treatment-related-change categories with mixed diagnostic support (1). It therefore permits investigation of a broad examination-level distinction, while retaining uncertainty about individual source diagnoses and the interpretation of treatment-change subtypes.

Quantitative image analysis offers a way to describe these examinations through explicit computations. Intensity statistics summarize the distribution of values within a region, whereas texture descriptors characterize aspects of gray-level organization that are not captured by a mean alone. Radiomics research has explored such information for differentiating progression and treatment effects in glioma, although the interpretation depends on the images, segmentation and feature definitions used (20). The Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative provides common definitions that help make feature calculations comparable (4). Standard definitions do not remove acquisition and preprocessing differences. In a separate task involving molecular glioma subtype classification, Foltyn-Dumitru and colleagues showed that intensity normalization affected external generalization, illustrating the importance of examining the processing pipeline rather than assuming portability from internal performance (22). That result does not establish the best transformation for post-treatment diagnosis. For the present question, a small, fixed set of fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) and postcontrast T1 descriptors provides a tractable computational representation. Its value lies in testing additional information under defined conditions; the descriptors are not direct measurements of viable tumor, microscopic tissue composition or a specific treatment-injury mechanism.

Clinical and imaging information may contribute different, partly overlapping signals. Age, treatment history and the interval from an initial clinical event describe the examination context, while MRI descriptors summarize the supplied image region. Combining them at the feature level can test whether imaging improves on an explicit clinical comparator. Existing studies support investigating this possibility without guaranteeing an increment. Zhou and colleagues reported favorable results from a model combining radiomics with clinical, molecular and advanced MRI information (6). McKenney and colleagues also reported better classification accuracy for a model combining imaging information with O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation status in a selected cohort with histopathological confirmation (7). Conversely, Leone and colleagues did not demonstrate a reliable improvement from adding diffusion, perfusion and radiomic information to their reference clinical model (9). The new-cohort evaluation by de Godoy and colleagues further illustrates why outcome grouping and reference definitions must be examined when interpreting model performance (10). These studies differ in patient selection, available sequences and diagnostic targets, so their areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUCs) cannot serve as a ranking of interchangeable methods. For a public-data analysis with a restricted input panel, the relevant question is the paired gain over its own clinical baseline.

A useful contribution in this setting is a transparent assessment of incremental information and its dependence on analytical choices. CLEAR emphasizes reporting acquisition, segmentation, feature extraction and model evaluation in enough detail for appraisal and reproduction (23). PROBAST+AI separately draws attention to data sources, predictors, outcomes and analysis when judging model quality, bias and applicability (24). These concerns apply to penalized regression as well as more complex learning algorithms. Evaluation guidance distinguishes performance estimated within the development data source from evaluation in independent data (25). Where patients contribute repeated examinations, this distinction also requires attention to dependence between observations: separating scans alone does not ensure that patients are separated between training and testing. Experiments with duplicated scans in connectome-based prediction demonstrate one way that leakage can inflate performance, although that task and duplication mechanism differ from longitudinal glioma imaging (14). A focused analysis should therefore compare models on the same examinations and patient-grouped folds, retain unfavorable sensitivity results, and report the scope of uncertainty. Transparent reporting, as encouraged by TRIPOD+AI, allows readers to judge what such internal evidence can support (2).

We therefore evaluated whether eight fixed MRI descriptors add discriminatory information to clinical inputs in a secondary analysis of public UCSD-PTGBM data (1). The target was residual or recurrent tumor versus broad treatment-related change at the examination level. We compared an age-and-sex model, an expanded clinical model, an MRI-only model and their feature-level fusion using the same available imaging cohort and patient-grouped validation. The primary comparison was the paired AUC difference between Fusion and the expanded clinical model. Probability error, calibration and class-specific classification were evaluated alongside discrimination. Boundary erosion, repeated grouped partitions and selection of one available examination per patient examined distinct aspects of sensitivity. By preserving the fixed feature panel and documenting processing and evaluation, the study aimed to characterize MRI's incremental contribution and its sensitivity to these analytical choices as a basis for independent assessment.
