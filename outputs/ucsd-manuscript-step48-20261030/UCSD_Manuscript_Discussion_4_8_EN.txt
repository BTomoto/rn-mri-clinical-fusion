The principal limitation is that discrimination was evaluated against inherited examination labels rather than independently readjudicated diagnoses. We assigned tumor status to the complement of the 51-case negative list, following the published binary partition. This reproduces the source grouping but cannot resolve individual diagnostic uncertainty. The dataset authors reported pathological confirmation for 45 of 192 tumor examinations in the full collection; this aggregate cannot establish the pathology-confirmed fraction of our imaging subset (1). Other source classifications incorporated clinical and imaging assessment. The target may therefore partly reflect the imaging appearances from which predictors were derived, creating possible dependence between the reference standard and the index data. Its direction and magnitude were not measured. Treatment-related change also combined pseudoprogression, radiation necrosis and nonspecific changes. Disagreement between workbook and published subtype counts remained unresolved despite agreement in the binary total, limiting subtype-specific interpretation. Annotation introduces a separate uncertainty. The source paper describes automated BraTS segmentation followed by radiologist correction and approval, whereas its cellular-tumor masks used clinical, pathological and longitudinal imaging information (1). We used the BraTS-region union, not those cellular-tumor masks; the information available during correction of the supplied BraTS masks could not be confirmed. Unverified blinding remains a potential bias concern rather than evidence that leakage occurred. Reporting guidance calls for documenting reference-standard methods and annotation sources (3). Patient grouping prevents cross-fold patient overlap but cannot repair these upstream uncertainties, which warrant independent diagnostic adjudication and documented annotation procedures in subsequent evaluation.
